The Review
This is an original submission that engages the theme of Amplification directly, and I recommend acceptance. Instead of using amplification as a metaphor, the paper builds an instrument that performs it: it takes the byte-level and encoding structure of text, which generative systems discard as noise, and treats it as the signal, giving each text a distinct body. The premise is clear and the paper delivers on it.

The three contributions are stated up front in the introduction, and the paper follows that structure consistently, which makes the scope easy to assess.

The theoretical framing is solid. The authors ground their notion of amplification in relevant work, from Drucker's argument that data should be treated as interpreted capta rather than given fact to Offenhuber's autographic design, and use it to support a specific claim: that the byte stream is an indexical trace of an encoding, a language, an input device, and an author, so amplifying structure means amplifying that trace rather than a meaning. The citations do real work. The framing of the machine as a co-creator rather than a mimetic parrot is also well motivated, but it is currently asserted more than argued. The paper implies a broader position, that such a system amplifies human expression rather than reproducing it, and a short discussion of how the same approach might transfer to other data streams or domains would give the contribution wider significance.

The treatment of bias is a strong point. The authors do not claim neutrality, they keep an explicit account of the biases the instrument introduces, noting that the movement vocabulary from AIST++ is culturally specific, that the equal treatment they claim holds only relative to that vocabulary, and that the amplification of multibyte scripts is a property of UTF-8 rather than any understanding of the languages. Disclosing bias rather than engineering it away is the right posture for a critical visualization and is consistent with the paper's argument.

On method, the pipeline is described in detail, and the choice of classical, interpretable techniques (PCA and K-Means for the codebook, a plausibility graph with Dijkstra bridging, and a physics smoothing layer) is well matched to the goal of a system an artist can read and inspect. The main issue is accessibility, VISAP addresses an audience that is not predominantly technical, and several passages, in particular the construction of the motion signature and the transition-cost heuristic for the plausibility graph, are dense enough that a non-specialist will lose the thread. The ideas themselves are intuitive; brief plain-language explanations alongside the formal description would make the method legible to the whole audience.

Three methodological choices would benefit from clarification, raised here as open questions. First, the mapping from byte value to motion region appears arbitrary: K-Means labels have no intrinsic ordering, so region 65 is not in any principled sense adjacent to region 66, and it is unclear whether the byte-to-region assignment follows the clustering labels or is ordered along a meaningful axis such as a principal component. This does not weaken the core claim, since identical structures still produce identical genomes, but it means the aesthetic character of a given dance may depend on labeling, while only the amount of movement, tied to byte count, is fully motivated. Second, using 256 regions gives one gesture per byte value, but that number is set by the encoding rather than by what best partitions the motion space, and acknowledging this tension would strengthen the account. Third, the paper reports quantitative measures (mean pairwise edit distance, entropy) over only seven texts, and the validation cites a mean edit distance of 32.5 over a "random sample" without specifying the population. Since the work declines to be a benchmark, these numbers are not load-bearing, but as stated they invite scrutiny the paper does not need; clarifying the sample and framing the metrics as illustrative would resolve this.

The composition of the corpus also needs clarification. The sentence introducing it, describing "a deliberately heterogeneous corpus spanning many themes: the loud Western canon, the overlooked exhaust of machines, historically marginalized voices, and scripts that digital infrastructure was never centered on", is compressed and hard to parse, and the rationale for the selection is left implicit. Since the case studies carry much of the argument, it would help to state why these registers were chosen, what each is meant to reveal, and how they map onto the categories in the results.

Overall, this is a well argued piece that fits the theme and is honest about its limitations. With a more accessible technical core, a clearer justification of the corpus, and a fuller development of the co-creator argument, it would be stronger. I recommend acceptance.
The Meta Review
Both reviewers converge on a Probably Accept assessment. There is broad agreement that the paper engages the Amplification theme directly and honestly, that the conceptual framing is solid, and that the technical implementation is coherent and well described. The panel recommends acceptance, while identifying several directions in which the camera-ready version could be strengthened.

The central strength is that the paper does not use amplification as a metaphor but builds an instrument that performs it, treating the byte-level and encoding structure of text, which generative systems typically discard as noise, as the signal that gives each text a distinct body. Both reviewers see this as an original and clearly stated research question, well supported by the writing and by the supplemental materials. The theoretical grounding in Drucker's argument for data as interpreted capta and in Offenhuber's autographic design does real work in support of the specific claim that the byte stream is an indexical trace of encoding, language, input device, and author. The treatment of bias is a further strength, since the paper does not claim neutrality but keeps an explicit account of the biases the instrument introduces, which is the right posture for a critical visualisation.

The concerns raised are consistent and are best read as directions for revision rather than as barriers to acceptance.

The technical core is dense for a mixed VISAP audience, and the construction of the motion signature and the transition-cost heuristic in particular would benefit from brief plain-language explanations placed alongside the formal description. Three methodological choices would also benefit from clarification. The mapping from byte value to motion region appears arbitrary, since K-Means labels have no intrinsic ordering, and it is unclear whether the assignment follows the clustering labels or an axis such as a principal component. The choice of 256 regions is set by the encoding rather than by what best partitions the motion space, and acknowledging this tension would strengthen the account. The quantitative measures are reported over only seven texts, and the "random sample" underlying the validation is not specified, so framing these numbers as illustrative and clarifying the sample would resolve the scrutiny they currently invite.

The composition of the corpus also needs to be made explicit. The compressed sentence introducing the "deliberately heterogeneous corpus" is hard to parse and leaves the rationale for the selection implicit, and since the case studies carry much of the argument, it would help to state why these registers were chosen and what each is meant to reveal. The framing of the machine as a co-creator rather than a mimetic parrot is currently asserted more than argued, and a short discussion of how the same approach might transfer to other data streams or domains would give the contribution wider significance. Finally, the artistic research dimension of the work would be considerably stronger with the involvement of real performers or dancers, since in its current form the animations read as visualisations of the code rather than as artistic outcomes.

Taken together, the panel regards this as an original, well-argued, and honestly limited submission with a clear fit to the theme. With a more accessible technical core, clarification of the methodological choices and the corpus, a fuller development of the co-creator argument, and at least a step toward embodied testing with performers, the paper would be stronger still. The recommendation is Probably Accept.
